# Argument patterns and diagnostics

Reusable scaffolds for the value/problem/movement layers. Project-specific failure→repair
examples belong in `project/lexicon.md`, not here.

## Value-map template

Write six compact statements before drafting or restructuring anything.

```text
Readers:                     Who specifically needs this result?
Shared belief/capability:    What do they already accept or already do?
Instability:                 What important relation is unresolved, contradictory, untestable?
Consequence of instability:  What does that block — explanation, prediction, measurement,
                             accumulation across studies?
This study's response:       What exactly does the work contribute?
Change for readers:          What can they understand or test differently afterward?
Evidence boundary:           What does the design not establish?
```

Reject generic formulations — "little is known," "no study has examined," "this topic is
important" — unless followed by a specific intellectual consequence. A gap is not a problem.
A problem is an instability in a community's understanding *with a cost*.

Then test the map against the design: **can this study actually deliver that value?** Narrow
the promise until claim and evidence match. Build the section sequence only after the value
claim survives that test.

## Introduction move sequence

1. Shared phenomenon, in the outer reader's terms.
2. Existing explanatory achievement — what the field can already account for.
3. The precise instability and its cost.
4. Candidate resolution and the linking proposition it depends on.
5. The design that can test that proposition.
6. Calibrated predictions or questions, with confirmatory/exploratory status stated.

## Discussion move sequence

1. Direct answer, sized to the evidence.
2. What changes theoretically or methodologically.
3. What the preferred interpretation does and does not rule out.
4. Four-validity boundary: construct, internal, statistical, external.
5. The specific next discriminating test.
6. The smallest important conclusion.

## Reverse outline before rewriting

For every paragraph, record:

| Paragraph | Function | Claim | Support | Reader transition | Keep/merge/split/delete |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Then act on it: merge paragraphs with duplicate functions, split paragraphs doing two
argumentative jobs, delete background that does not change the reader's route to the main
claim. A paragraph whose removal costs the argument nothing is not "context" — it is padding
that spends the reader's attention budget.

## Using the literature as a conversation

Organize prior work by its role in the instability, never as a list:

- what the community can already explain;
- where those explanations stop;
- competing interpretations;
- what evidence would discriminate between them or connect them.

Each source should change the logic of the problem. A citation that could be deleted without
altering the argument is decoration.

## Common failure → repair

- **Topic opening** — "X is ubiquitous." → Open with the operation or decision the study helps
  explain.
- **Gap worship** — "No study has used method Y." → State what the field cannot currently
  predict or test.
- **Theory parade** — one paragraph per theory. → Select one backbone; admit a second only for
  a distinct link or a rival prediction.
- **Method-first value** — "We developed tool Z." → Name the inference that becomes possible,
  then validate that inference.
- **Result inflation** — "X explains Y." → Name the specific measure, the association, its
  uncertainty, and the surviving alternatives.
- **Limitation dump** — a generic final paragraph. → Tie each limitation to the exact
  inference it weakens and the next test that would address it.

## Test value against evidence

Before endorsing a contribution sentence, ask:

- Does the study observe the proposed construct, or a proxy for it?
- Does the design distinguish the preferred explanation from simpler alternatives — exposure,
  time, salience, volume, selection?
- Is the inference across participants, time, stimuli, or all three?
- Would the claim remain interesting if the focal coefficient were null?
- What is the smallest conclusion that still changes how an outer reader thinks?
