# Drills

A diagnosis fixes one passage. Drills change a writer. Assign **two or three**, matched to the
patterns you named — a list of eight exercises gets skipped entirely.

Each drill states the habit it treats, what the author does, and how they know it worked. Give
the drill in their own terms; don't just paste the name.

The point of every one of these is the same: make the author *see* the pattern in their own
prose without you pointing at it. That's the skill that transfers to the next draft.

---

## 1. Subjects and verbs

**Treats:** nominalization clusters, hidden agents, abstract subjects.

Take one page. Underline the grammatical subject and main verb of every sentence. Read only
those pairs aloud.

You should hear characters doing things. If you hear "analysis was conducted," "consideration
is given," "the relationship exists" — the actors are missing, and the reader is reconstructing
them on your behalf.

**Done when:** most subject-verb pairs name someone or something doing something.

## 2. The first five words

**Treats:** old-before-new violations, choppy paragraphs, sentences with nothing to attach to.

Underline the first five words of every sentence in one paragraph. Each should contain
something from the sentence before — a repeated term, a pronoun pointing back, a summarizing
noun.

Where the link is missing, you've asked the reader to hold new material with nowhere to put it.

**Done when:** you can trace a chain of shared terms from the paragraph's first sentence to its
last.

## 3. The last four words

**Treats:** wasted stress positions, trailing citations, emphasis on qualifications.

Read only the final four words of every sentence in a key paragraph. That list is what your
reader carries forward.

If it's mostly citations, "in this context," and "to some extent," the emphasis positions are
being spent on furniture.

**Done when:** the list reads as the paragraph's substance.

## 4. The paragraph subject list

**Treats:** scattered paragraphs where every sentence is individually clear.

List the grammatical subject of every sentence in one paragraph. If the list contains more than
two or three distinct topics, the paragraph is doing more than one job — and a reader has no
way to say afterward what it was about.

Caveat worth respecting: a paragraph tracking a genuinely complex relation may shift subjects
deliberately. Judge by whether a reader could state the topic afterward, not by the count.

**Done when:** the subject list names one thing, or one thing and its parts.

## 5. Verb by word twelve

**Treats:** long opening clauses, delayed verbs, suspended judgment.

Find every sentence where the main verb arrives after word 12. For each, ask what is sitting in
front of it and whether that material could follow the verb instead.

**Done when:** the reader learns what the subject is *doing* before they've forgotten what the
subject was.

## 6. The deletion test for openers

**Treats:** throat-clearing, metadiscourse that substitutes for the claim.

Delete every "It is important to note that," "It should be emphasized that," "There are several
factors which," and "In order to" from one section. Read it again and mark which sentences lost
anything.

Keep the signposting that helps a reader navigate a long document — "I turn now to the second
condition" earns its space. Cut the openers that announce a claim instead of making it.

**Done when:** the surviving openers all carry content.

## 7. The hedge audit

**Treats:** hedge stacking, claims the author doesn't sound willing to make.

Find every sentence with two or more hedges. For each, ask: what exactly am I uncertain about?
Keep the hedge that names that specific limit; drop the rest.

Some venues require heavy modality — check what comparable published work does before cutting
on principle. What is never idiomatic anywhere is the stack.

**Done when:** every remaining hedge marks a limit you could defend if asked.

## 8. The claim in twenty words

**Treats:** overlong central claims, buried thesis, no sentence that lands.

Write the passage's main claim in twenty words or fewer, as a single sentence, with no hedges.
Then decide which hedges to add back and name what each one is protecting against.

**Done when:** the twenty-word version is defensible and you can say precisely what each
restored qualifier does.

## 9. The hidden agent hunt

**Treats:** passives that conceal choices the reader needs to evaluate.

For every passive construction in your results and discussion, ask whether the hidden agent is
you. "Three sites were excluded" hides who excluded them and why — precisely what a skeptical
reader wants.

Leave the passives where the recipient is genuinely the topic, or the agent genuinely doesn't
matter. Methods sections are full of correct passives.

**Done when:** no passive in the draft hides a decision you made.
