# Problem framing

A paper that reports work is read by the author's advisor. A paper that changes what the reader
believes is read by the field. The difference is not prose and not coverage: it is whether the first
two hundred words establish that something the reader currently relies on no longer works.

## 1. Name the problem, not the topic

Write two sentences before drafting anything:

- **What the reader currently believes or does.** Not "the literature has studied X" -- the actual
  operative assumption. "Investigators treat a rising network measure as evidence that viewers track
  narrative structure, and a dozen published findings rest on that reading."
- **Why it is now inadequate.** A claim the reader would resist losing, with the reason it fails. "Any
  quantity that accumulates as a story unfolds rises with the clock, so every one of those findings is
  equally consistent with viewers tracking nothing but elapsed time."

If the second sentence could be replaced by "but nobody has studied Y", you have a topic, not a
problem. Keep working.

## 2. Write into the reader's existing framework

The reader arrives with a code -- a working model of the field. You are not filling a hole in it; you
are showing which part of it must change. So say which part, and make the contradiction explicit:

> The standard account holds that **A**. If **A** were right, **B** would follow. It does not: **C**.

Three sentences, and the reader now has a stake in your result before you have reported one. Without
them, the reader has to construct the stake themselves, and readers do not do that.

## 3. Make the reader's work small

Readers are busy, self-interested, and reading for their own purposes. They will not infer why your
finding matters. So:

- The value goes in the **first two sentences** of the abstract and of every section, not at the end
  of a paragraph.
- A section heading states the finding: "Much of that association is the clock", not "Results".
- Never write "this is important because" and then supply the reason three sentences later. The reason
  is the sentence.

## 4. Value is instability, not coverage

Nobody is rewarded for reading a complete survey. They are rewarded for learning that something they
relied on is unstable. So the literature review is not a survey of who studied what: it is the
argument that the reader's current position has a crack in it, and here it is.

Every paragraph of the introduction should raise or tighten that instability. A paragraph that only
establishes that you read widely is doing no work and should be cut.

## 5. The self is not the subject

"We believe", "I argue", "our view is" put the author between the reader and the problem. The problem
is the subject of every sentence in the opening:

- Weak: "We argue that existing measures are confounded."
- Right: "Existing measures are confounded, and the confound is not a technicality."

## 6. Significance is a claim about the reader's next move

The last paragraph is not a summary of the paper. It states what a reader who accepts your result can
now do, or must now stop doing. "We found X in three films" is a summary. "Anyone who treats an
accumulating network measure as evidence of tracking must first show it diverges from the clock" is a
claim about the reader's future behaviour, and it is the sentence they carry away.

## The two-minute check

Read only the first two sentences of the abstract, then the first sentence of each paragraph in the
introduction. If those sentences do not, in order, (a) name what the reader believes, (b) show why it
fails, (c) say what you measured, and (d) say what follows for the reader -- rewrite the opening
before touching anything else.
